\begin{abstract}
 Large language models are sensitive to the coordinate system used to express a multiple-choice problem: reordering answer options can change the emitted label even when the underlying question is unchanged. Standard self-consistency treats such outputs as samples in one label space and therefore cannot distinguish semantic agreement from coordinate agreement. We study a controlled alternative, \ro{} inference, which applies the exact inverse of a known option permutation to every transformed-view answer before aggregating a smoothed categorical distribution. In a preregistered shared-question block, the method improved the fixed-model average across Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B by 3.13 percentage points (95\% bootstrap interval [0.17, 5.90]) and exceeded randomized maps by 34.67 points. A frozen extension to Phi-3.5-mini, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Gemma-2-2B produced positive point estimates for all three models and a fixed-model average gain of 3.65 points (95\% interval [0.87, 6.60]), although its strict individual-replication gate was not passed. Two independent follow-up blocks on ARC-Challenge, OpenBookQA and MMLU increased the pooled evidence to 384 paired questions: mapped inference improved accuracy by 7.29 points over root inference (95\% interval [2.60, 11.98]) and exceeded randomized maps by 30.29 points. The evidence supports positive average transfer across the tested fixed panel, not universal benefit for every model, benchmark or transformation; mapped test-time reinforcement-learning (TTRL) rewards were positive on an easy synthetic family but inconclusive on a real-benchmark diagnostic.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Multiple-choice evaluation is attractive for language-model research because it provides a discrete decision target, reproducible scoring and broad coverage of reasoning and knowledge tasks. Yet the target is not only a semantic choice. A model must also emit a coordinate label such as ``A'' or ``D'', and the relationship between the semantic option and that label changes when the options are permuted. Empirical studies have documented substantial sensitivity to option order and position, even when the question content is held fixed \citep{zheng2023selectors,pezeshkpour2024order}. This creates a failure mode for test-time aggregation: two answers can be semantically identical while carrying different surface labels, or can agree in label while referring to different options.

For example, suppose the canonical answer is option 2, and a cyclic rotation moves that option to position 0. A model that emits ``0'' for the rotated prompt has selected the same semantic answer as a model that emits ``2'' for the canonical prompt. Pooling the two raw labels treats them as disagreement; applying the known inverse rotation converts both observations to canonical option 2 before aggregation. This distinction between semantic identity and label identity is the object tested here.

Self-consistency addresses a related problem by sampling multiple reasoning paths and marginalizing their answers \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. Its usual aggregation, however, assumes that all samples already inhabit a common answer coordinate system. A prompt ensemble that changes option order violates that assumption. Permutation self-consistency has also been studied for listwise ranking, where reordered candidate lists are aggregated to reduce order effects \citep{tang2024middle}; listwise marginalization does not, by itself, provide the exact inverse map needed for a multiple-choice answer label.

Here we test whether known relational structure can be used as an inference-time alignment operation. We generate matched samples from an identity prompt and three cyclic option rotations. Each transformed answer is mapped back to the canonical option index before a full-support log-opinion pool is formed. The method is deliberately narrow: it assumes a known, finite option permutation and does not claim to solve arbitrary prompt inconsistency. The central question is whether exact alignment improves accuracy beyond root-only self-consistency when the number of generated completions is held fixed.

We evaluate this question in an evidence ladder. A CPU simulator tests the intended recovery mechanism and coherently wrong controls. A Qwen2.5-3B model is evaluated on synthetic arithmetic and three public benchmarks, followed by a disjoint Qwen2.5-7B confirmation. We then run a shared-question block across Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B, and a further fixed panel of Phi-3.5-mini, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 and Gemma-2-2B. The latter panel was frozen before candidate generations and tests transfer across five model families. We also report compute scaling, hard-vote and randomized-map controls, label-free selective risk, and matched-compute TTRL adaptation diagnostics.

The contribution has two parts: an auditable inference-time estimator that canonicalizes transformed answers before pooling, and an empirical characterization of when that estimator helps or fails. The resulting claim is bounded: the method shows positive average transfer across the tested fixed model panel, while medium-difficulty arithmetic, affine transformations, some high-ceiling benchmark blocks and real-benchmark policy adaptation define important limits.

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Self-consistency and test-time sampling.}
Self-consistency samples multiple reasoning paths and aggregates their final answers, improving chain-of-thought reasoning without changing model parameters \citep{wang2023selfconsistency}. Our method retains the matched-compute sampling principle but changes the order of operations: a known transformation is inverted before the samples are pooled. This makes the transformation part of the estimator rather than an unmodelled source of output variation.

\paragraph{Multiple-choice order sensitivity.}
Large language models are not robust multiple-choice selectors, and their answer distributions can depend on option position \citep{zheng2023selectors}. Controlled studies of option-order sensitivity provide a direct motivation for treating answer labels as coordinates rather than semantic objects \citep{pezeshkpour2024order}. We use the same kind of option reorderings as a controlled intervention, but retain the exact correspondence between transformed and canonical options for aggregation.

\paragraph{Permutation-aware aggregation.}
Permutation self-consistency improves listwise ranking by marginalizing over candidate-list orderings \citep{tang2024middle}. The present setting differs in both object and operation: the output is a four-way answer label, the transformations form a known cyclic action, and the inverse action is applied to each sampled label before probability pooling. Randomized inverse maps and unmapped-view baselines separate this relational signal from diversity generated by merely changing the prompt.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Task formulation and relational views}

For a question with four canonical options, let the answer space be
\[
    \mathcal{A}=\{\varnothing,0,1,2,3\},
\]
where $\varnothing$ denotes a parser failure and the integer indices denote the four canonical options. Let $G=C_4$ be the cyclic group of option rotations. For each $g\in G$, the question is rendered with options transformed by $g$, and the model emits $R$ sampled labels $y_{g,1},\ldots,y_{g,R}$. The known inverse map $g^{-1}$ sends a valid transformed label back to its canonical index and leaves $\varnothing$ unchanged.

The experimental pipeline is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}. The identity view is retained as the root coordinate system. The three non-identity views are generated with the same question stem and answer content, but with cyclically shifted option positions. The transformations disperse every fixed-position shortcut over all four canonical candidates.

\subsection{Canonical log-opinion pooling}

For each view, mapped sample counts are smoothed over the complete support. With $n_g(a)$ occurrences of answer $a$ among $R$ samples and smoothing constant $\alpha=1$,
\[
 p_g(a)=\frac{n_g(a)+\alpha}{R+\alpha|\mathcal{A}|}.
\]
The canonical log-opinion pool is
\[
 q(a)=\frac{\exp\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\log p_g(a)\right)}
 {\sum_{b\in\mathcal{A}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\log p_g(b)\right)}.
\]
The prediction is $\arg\max_{a\in\{0,1,2,3\}}q(a)$; the parse-failure symbol remains in the normalization and is never silently discarded. Ties use the lowest answer index.

The matched-compute root baseline uses $|G|R=16$ samples from the identity prompt and majority vote. The unmapped baseline pools transformed labels without applying inverse maps. For the randomized-map control, the identity inverse is retained and each non-identity inverse is composed with an independently sampled random permutation of the four labels. Thus the control preserves the generated text and the generation budget while destroying the known relational correspondence.

\subsection{Test-time reinforcement-learning adaptation diagnostic}

The parameter-updating experiments use the same views but replace the final pooled prediction with leave-one-out soft rewards. For a rollout $r$ in view $g$, its reward is the pooled probability assigned to its mapped answer after that rollout is removed from its own view. Rewards are centered within each prompt. The policy update maximizes the full completion sequence log-probability weighted by the centered reward and adds a KL penalty to the frozen reference model:
\[
 \mathcal{L}= -\frac{1}{B}\sum_i A_i\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)
 +\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),
\]
with low-rank adaptation (LoRA) rank 8 \citep{hu2022lora}, learning rate $10^{-5}$, $\beta=0.02$, one on-policy pass and target modules $q_{\mathrm{proj}}$ and $v_{\mathrm{proj}}$. Root test-time reinforcement learning (TTRL) receives the same sequence budget on the identity prompt. These experiments are reported as exploratory because the real-benchmark diagnostic was not decisive.

\subsection{Label-free reliability}

For each mapped prediction, we recompute the prediction after omitting each transformed view in turn. The primary stability score is the fraction of leave-one-view-out predictions equal to the full prediction. A secondary margin is the difference between the two largest pooled answer probabilities. Examples are ranked by decreasing stability, then margin, then content hash, without using labels. Accuracy and risk are reported at 25\%, 50\%, 75\% and 100\% coverage.

\section{Experimental design and statistical analysis}

\subsection{Models, benchmarks and compute}

All language-model experiments used FP16 inference on one NVIDIA RTX 3090 with 24 GB memory. Unless otherwise stated, prompts used temperature 0.8, top-$p$ 0.95, maximum 192 new tokens, four cyclic views and four samples per view. The primary benchmarks were ARC-Challenge validation \citep{clark2018arc}, OpenBookQA validation \citep{mihaylov2018openbookqa} and MMLU test \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, restricted to eligible four-option questions. Dataset snapshots were mirrored locally, fingerprinted and recorded in every manifest.

The initial public-benchmark transfer used Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct; a disjoint content-hash block was used for Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025technical}. The shared block then evaluated Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct \citep{dubey2024llama} on the same 192 questions. A separate frozen panel evaluated Phi-3.5-mini-Instruct \citep{abdin2024phi}, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \citep{jiang2023mistral} and Gemma-2-2B-it \citep{team2024gemma2} on that same shared block. The panel was frozen before any candidate output was inspected.

\subsection{Question blocks and preregistration}

Question selection was deterministic using content hashes. The initial 3B and 7B transfer blocks, the adaptation training and evaluation blocks, and the original Llama block were disjoint. The shared block removed all prior hashes before selecting 64 questions per benchmark with seed 20260817. The later three-model panel reused this shared block to estimate model-family transfer at fixed questions; it did not change prompts, parser, maps or thresholds after candidate inference.

\subsection{Primary estimands and uncertainty}

For every question, the primary paired contrast was mapped correctness minus matched-compute root correctness. The map-control contrast was mapped correctness minus the mean correctness of 20 randomized inverse-map controls generated from the same transformed samples. Intervals were paired bootstrap intervals. For shared blocks, questions were resampled with replacement within benchmark strata and the same sampled indices were applied to every fixed model. For the cross-family panel, 10,000 bootstrap replicates were used. The model checkpoints are fixed estimands, not random draws from the space of all language models.

The shared-block paper gate required a positive fixed-model-average interval, at least two of three nonnegative model effects, no model interval wholly below zero, a positive map-control interval with effect at least 5 points, truth support at least 0.80 and parse failure at most 0.10 for every model, plus successful audits. The cross-family panel added a stricter requirement that at least one new model independently exceed 3 points with an interval excluding zero. Failed gates were retained and did not trigger tuning on their evaluation questions.

\section{Results}

We organize the results around one question: when does canonicalizing option-permuted views improve a fixed-compute decision? We first test the recovery mechanism, then establish the primary transfer effect and its cross-model boundary, and finally report secondary diagnostics and failure cases.

\subsection{Mechanism and synthetic recovery}

The CPU testbed first isolated the statistical mechanism from language-model behavior. With 10,000 simulated orbits, dispersed shortcut errors produced a mapped accuracy of 1.000 versus 0.125 for root-only inference (a +87.5-point contrast). A stable shortcut produced 0.020 mapped accuracy versus 0.125 root accuracy, and a coherently wrong regime produced zero accuracy for both methods. Thus mapping can repair errors when view-specific shortcuts disperse, but cannot repair a coherent wrong mode.

The easy synthetic Qwen2.5-3B inference pilot reproduced the predicted recovery effect. In the confirmatory 64-orbit block, mapped pooling improved accuracy from 0.547 to 0.813 (+26.56 points, 95\% CI [+15.63, +39.06]); an independent 64-orbit replication improved accuracy from 0.578 to 0.828 (+25.00 points, CI [+12.50, +37.50]). Correct maps exceeded randomized gauges by 43.44 and 44.30 points, respectively. Truth-support coverage was at least 0.969 and parse failure was approximately 2\% in both runs.

The preregistered medium-difficulty gate was intentionally harder: each arithmetic problem used two or three operations and moduli 11, 13, 17 or 19. One seed gave +12.50 points and the other gave -1.56 points; the pooled effect was +5.47 points, but its interval included zero and the map-control threshold was not met. A post-gate easy reference reproduced a +31.25-point mapped gain, ruling out environment drift. We therefore interpret the medium result as a capability boundary rather than an implementation failure.

The transformation-family characterization further bounded the claim. Boolean negation was retained as a negative control because the correct-map advantage over randomized maps was -0.94 points. Affine transformations beat randomized gauges but harmed root-only accuracy, with mapped-minus-root effects of -6.25 and -18.75 points for the two affine families. Exact semantic validity of a map therefore does not guarantee a positive net accuracy effect.

\subsection{Primary transfer to public benchmarks and model scale}

The first public-benchmark transfer used 64 questions per benchmark and matched 16 total generations per question. On Qwen2.5-3B, mapped pooling improved ARC-Challenge from 0.469 to 0.750 (+28.13 points, CI [+15.63, +40.63]), OpenBookQA from 0.750 to 0.859 (+10.94 points, CI [+4.69, +18.75]) and MMLU from 0.391 to 0.531 (+14.06 points, CI [+1.56, +26.56]). The pooled improvement was +17.71 points (CI [+10.94, +24.48]). Correct maps exceeded randomized gauges by 35.6 points in the pooled block.

To test whether this effect persisted outside the first transfer sample, we preregistered two independent follow-up blocks on the same three benchmarks. Each block used 64 questions per benchmark, a new sample offset and a new run seed, with no tuning or selective stopping. Across the two blocks (384 paired questions in total), root accuracy was 56.77\% and mapped accuracy was 64.06\%, for a +7.29-point gain (95\% CI [+2.60, +11.98]). The benchmark-specific point estimates were +9.38 points on ARC-Challenge, +6.25 on OpenBookQA and +6.25 on MMLU; only the pooled interval excludes zero. Correct maps exceeded randomized maps by +30.29 points (95\% CI [+24.80, +36.08]). These follow-up results provide an independent precision check rather than a replacement for the original transfer gate.

Qwen2.5-7B was evaluated on a disjoint 64-question block per benchmark with unchanged prompts and sampling. The pooled gain was smaller but positive: root accuracy was 0.797 and mapped accuracy was 0.849, a +5.21-point difference with CI [+1.56, +8.85]. The reduced effect is compatible with a ceiling effect: the root baseline was already high, while the correct-map advantage over randomized gauges remained +40.4 points.

\subsection{Shared-question confirmation and cross-family transfer}

The paper-scale shared block used the same 192 questions for Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B. The fixed-model average mapped-minus-root effect was +3.13 points (95\% CI [+0.17, +5.90]), and the correct-map-minus-randomized effect was +34.67 points (CI [+28.38, +40.97]). Qwen2.5-3B showed +7.29 points (CI [+0.52, +14.06]), Qwen2.5-7B +2.60 points (CI [-1.04, +6.25]) and Llama-3.2-3B -0.52 points (CI [-5.21, +4.17]). The shared-block gate passed because the fixed-model interval was positive, no model interval was wholly below zero, the map control was positive, and all data and numerical audits passed. The Llama effect remains unresolved rather than positive evidence for universal family transfer.

We then evaluated three additional model families on the same frozen question block. All three point estimates were positive: Phi-3.5-mini +2.08 points (CI [-1.04, +5.21]), Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 +5.21 points (CI [-0.52, +10.94]) and Gemma-2-2B +3.65 points (CI [-1.04, +8.33]). Their fixed-model average was +3.65 points (CI [+0.87, +6.60]), and correct maps exceeded randomized maps by +34.23 points (CI [+27.92, +40.76]). Truth support ranged from 0.943 to 0.979 and parse failure from 0.005 to 0.010. Structural audits, overlap checks, per-view symmetry checks and matched-token checks passed for all three models.

The strict cross-family gate was not passed: none of the three individual intervals excluded zero. This criterion was deliberately stronger than a positive panel average. The result is therefore best described as positive average transfer across a diverse fixed panel, with insufficient evidence for an individually confirmed effect in every new family.

\subsection{Secondary analyses: compute scaling, pooling and reliability}

The stored generations allowed a prespecified N=4/8/16 scaling analysis without additional model calls. In the new panel, the low-budget N=4 mapped-minus-root effects were +5.21 points for Phi-3.5-mini (CI [+1.04, +9.90]), +8.33 points for Mistral-7B (CI [+2.08, +14.58]) and +2.60 points for Gemma-2-2B (CI [-3.13, +8.33]). At N=16, the corresponding effects were +2.08, +5.21 and +3.65 points. The Qwen and Llama panels showed the same qualitative ceiling dependence in several blocks, although not uniformly. These results suggest that relational alignment is most useful when the root baseline has limited sampling budget; N=4 and N=8 remain secondary analyses.

Hard vote and log-opinion pooling were close in many blocks, but their differences were not consistently positive. For example, on the new panel the N=16 hard-vote effects were +1.56 points for Phi, -0.52 for Mistral and +1.04 for Gemma relative to log pooling. The main estimator was therefore retained as the full-support log-opinion pool, while hard vote was reported as an ablation rather than selected post hoc.

Label-free reliability showed that stable predictions were substantially more accurate at selective coverage. At 50\% coverage, mapped accuracy was 96.9\% for Phi-3.5-mini, 93.8\% for Mistral-7B and 92.7\% for Gemma-2-2B. Mean leave-one-view-out agreement was 0.957, 0.897 and 0.930, respectively. The analogous 50\% accuracies for Qwen2.5-3B, Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.2-3B were 92.7\%, 97.9\% and 95.8\%. These analyses are descriptive and use no labels for ranking or threshold selection.

\subsection{Failure boundaries and exploratory adaptation}

The easy synthetic adaptation study used five independent seeds, 16 unlabeled training orbits and 512 sequences per method per seed. Across 304 fresh evaluation orbits, mapped TTRL exceeded matched-compute root TTRL by +5.65 points under an equal-seed estimate; a hierarchical bootstrap interval was [+2.3, +9.1]. All five seed-level effects were positive. The mapped method also exceeded the unadapted base by +7.03 points with hierarchical CI [+3.4, +10.9]. Training KL remained finite and small.

The real-benchmark adaptation diagnostic used 18 unlabeled training questions and 96 untouched evaluation questions. Mapped TTRL exceeded root TTRL by +2.08 points, but the paired interval was [-1.04, +5.21]. The benchmark-specific effects were -0.78 points on ARC-Challenge, +3.91 points on OpenBookQA and +3.13 points on MMLU. Because its prespecified interval included zero, no multi-seed real-benchmark adaptation expansion was run.

The transformation and difficulty controls define a second boundary. The medium synthetic gate failed despite a positive map-control point estimate, and affine transformations often reduced root accuracy after mapping. These negative results rule out the stronger statement that any valid relational transformation or any harder reasoning distribution will benefit from pooling.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments support a simple interpretation: when model errors contain a coordinate-dependent component, exact inverse alignment converts several transformed views into evidence about one semantic answer. The randomized-map controls are important here. Transformed prompts alone were not sufficient; the known map produced a large and consistent advantage over random inverse gauges across Qwen, Llama, Phi, Mistral and Gemma panels.

The effect was heterogeneous. Qwen2.5-3B had the largest initial public-benchmark gain, while Qwen2.5-7B and the new families had smaller primary N=16 effects. The independent follow-up blocks reduced uncertainty for the pooled Qwen2.5-3B estimate and produced a confidence interval excluding zero, but benchmark-level intervals for OpenBookQA and MMLU remained wider. The low-budget scaling results indicate why: when root self-consistency already has high accuracy, there is less room for a view-alignment method to repair errors. Conversely, the weaker Mistral and Gemma baselines left more room for mapped pooling, but their question-level intervals were still too wide to establish an individually significant effect at the fixed block size.

The experiments also distinguish inference-time alignment from policy adaptation. Mapped rewards improved easy synthetic adaptation across seeds, but the real-benchmark diagnostic did not exclude zero. The policy update introduces additional sources of variance, including sparse training questions, model-specific optimization behavior and a mismatch between the training reward and final benchmark accuracy. We therefore treat TTRL as a secondary direction rather than the central contribution.

Several limitations are intrinsic to the study. All public tasks use four-option multiple choice, and the maps are cyclic option permutations. The candidate model panel is fixed rather than sampled from the population of all language models. The cross-family panel reuses the shared question block, which is appropriate for matched model-transfer estimation but does not test transfer to new task content. Some benchmark-model pairs showed negative mapped-minus-root differences, including Llama on the shared block and Gemma on MMLU. The current study also does not provide a unified head-to-head comparison with every existing permutation-aware aggregation method. Finally, no result supports universal improvement across transformations: the affine controls provide explicit counterexamples.

The practical implication is narrower and more useful than a universal claim. Exact relational alignment is a parameter-free inference wrapper that can improve average matched-compute multiple-choice accuracy and can provide a label-free confidence signal for selective prediction, although it increases generation cost by evaluating multiple prompt views. Its use should therefore be accompanied by task-specific validation, a randomized-map control, an explicit compute budget and comparison with the strongest available permutation-aware baseline. The current evidence motivates such validation rather than replacing it.

\section{Conclusion}

We introduced an auditable inference-time procedure that maps option-permuted model outputs into a shared canonical space before pooling. Across synthetic recovery tests, public benchmarks, Qwen scale confirmation, cross-family panels and two independent follow-up blocks, exact alignment produced positive average effects and large advantages over randomized maps. The 384-question pooled follow-up estimate was +7.29 points with a 95\% interval [+2.60, +11.98]. The evidence is strongest for inference-time use under constrained sampling budgets; TTRL adaptation remains exploratory. The method does not improve every model-task pair, does not transfer automatically to affine transformations or harder reasoning distributions, and does not justify a universal model-family claim.

\section*{Methods availability and reproducibility}

The repository contains frozen protocols, source code, test suites, raw parsed records, manifests, environment descriptions and model-output audit reports.
